# AWWA and WRF Predictive Maintenance Guidance

## 1. Industry Context and Current State

### Most Utilities: Reactive Reality

Reactive maintenance means repairing equipment only after it fails. Preventive maintenance (PM) means servicing equipment on a fixed calendar schedule regardless of condition. Predictive maintenance (PdM) means using sensor data and analysis to detect problems early and act before failure.

| Parameter | Typical Current State |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Planned work percentage | 46-50% (reactive work dominates) |
| PM compliance | 50-60% of scheduled PMs completed on time |
| Maintenance cost as % of asset replacement value | 3-5% |
| Wrench time | 25-35% of technician day on hands-on tasks |
| Maintenance approach | Run-to-failure, calendar-based PM |
| Data integration | Spreadsheets, disconnected systems |
| Workforce median age | 48 years, 30-50% eligible to retire in 5-10 years |

Industry benchmarks from SMRP (Society for Maintenance and Reliability Professionals), AWWA (American Water Works Association), and DOE/FEMP (Department of Energy / Federal Energy Management Program) show that average utilities operate at 50-60% planned work. Planned work percentage means the share of maintenance hours that are scheduled in advance rather than spent on emergency repairs. Organizations below 50% are classified as reactive. Among 545 U.S. water utilities surveyed by AWWA in 2015, reactive maintenance was the dominant operating model.

### What Is Possible with Better Data Integration

| Parameter | World-Class Target |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Planned work percentage | Greater than 90% |
| PM compliance | Greater than 92% within +/- 10% timing window |
| Maintenance cost as % of asset replacement value | 2-3% |
| Wrench time | Greater than 55% |
| Equipment availability | Greater than 95% |
| Maintenance overtime | Less than 5% |
| Training investment | 4% of total labor costs |
| Planner-to-technician ratio | 1:20 to 1:30 |
| Supervisor-to-technician ratio | 1:10 to 1:15 |

Wrench time is the portion of a technician's paid day spent with tools in hand performing maintenance tasks. The rest is spent traveling, retrieving parts, completing paperwork, or waiting. 
Top-performing facilities demonstrate that less than 10% reactive work is achievable. DOE/FEMP data shows predictive maintenance yields 30-40% savings over reactive approaches and 8-12% savings over preventive maintenance alone. These savings come from reduced overtime, fewer emergency repairs, and longer equipment life.

## 2. EPA Effective Utility Management (EUM) Framework

The EUM framework was developed in 2008 by EPA (Environmental Protection Agency), AWWA, WEF (Water Environment Federation), NACWA (National Association of Clean Water Agencies), and seven other organizations. It was most recently updated in August 2024. It is described as the most widely used water utility management framework in the United States.

### Ten Attributes of Effectively Managed Water Sector Utilities

Each attribute describes a characteristic that a well-run water utility should demonstrate. Internally, the attributes serve as a self-assessment checklist: a utility can rate itself on each attribute to identify gaps.

| Attribute | Focus |
|-----------|-------|
| Community Sustainability | Active leadership role in community sustainability through collaboration with local partners |
| Customer Experience and Satisfaction | Reliable and responsive services aligned with customer-derived service levels |
| Enterprise Resiliency | Risk management for enterprise continuity, including cybersecurity, natural disasters, and talent loss |
| Financial Viability | Predictable rates covering full life-cycle costs, reserves, and investment for future needs |
| Infrastructure Strategy and Performance | Asset condition understanding, lowest life-cycle cost, asset management plan aligned to strategic business plan |
| Operational Optimization | Continuous incorporation of innovative solutions, effective use of data management and analysis tools |
| Regulatory and Reliability Performance | Meets or exceeds regulatory requirements, provides reliable services |
| Stakeholder Understanding and Support | Enables understanding of service levels, rate structures, and capital improvement programs |
| Water Resources Sustainability | Integrated water resource management considering all water of value |
| Workforce Development | Recruits, develops, retains, and inspires an inclusive, competent, motivated workforce |

### Five Keys to Management Success

| Key | Definition |
|-----|-----------|
| Continual Improvement Management | Plan-Do-Check-Act framework. Includes self-assessment, performance measures, gap analysis, and change management |
| Knowledge Management | SOPs (standard operating procedures), workforce planning, data integration. Treats knowledge as an asset to be managed |
| Leadership | Required at every level. Drives Organizational Culture and embodies commitment to purposeful change |
| Measurement | Basis for management improvement. Performance measures at strategic, tactical, and operational levels |
| Strategic Business Planning and Management | Long-term plan reflecting community values, driving utility objectives, measurement efforts, and investments |

### EPA Five Core Questions for Asset Management

These five questions form the foundation of any asset management program. Asset management means systematically managing infrastructure to minimize life-cycle cost while meeting service levels. Internally, every decision about maintenance, repair, or replacement should trace back to one of these questions.

| Question | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| What is the current state of my assets? | Asset registry, condition assessment, remaining useful life estimation |
| What is my required sustainable level of service? | Performance standards matching customer and regulatory expectations |
| Which assets are critical to sustained performance? | Criticality analysis, consequence of failure assessment |
| What are my minimum life-cycle costs? | O&M plus capital renewal optimized over asset life |
| What is my best long-term funding strategy? | Revenue requirements, rate structures, capital planning |

The America's Water Infrastructure Act (AWIA) of 2018 codified these core questions into federal law. This means water systems are now legally required to incorporate asset management planning. States must amend Capacity Development strategies to encourage asset management plans incorporating these questions. Community water systems serving over 3,300 people must conduct Risk and Resilience Assessments. Non-compliance penalties reach $25,000 per day.

## 3. Maturity Progression: Reactive to Predictive

### DOE/FEMP Maintenance Approach Benchmarks

These percentages represent how a utility divides its maintenance effort across three categories. Reactive means fixing things after they break. Preventive means servicing on a fixed schedule. Predictive means using data to detect problems before failure. A utility moving from reactive to predictive gradually shifts these percentages.

| Maintenance Approach | Typical Industry Range | Best-in-Class Range | Target Goal Range |
|---------------------|----------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Reactive | 40-60% | Less than 10% | 20-30% |
| Preventive | 30-50% | 25-35% | 25-35% |
| Predictive | 10-15% | 45-55% | 30-40% |

### Maintenance Maturity Levels

Maturity levels describe how advanced a utility's maintenance program is. Level 1 (Reactive) means no planning. Level 5 (World-Class) means predictive analytics drive all decisions. Most utilities start at Level 1 or 2.

| Level | Name | Planned Work % | PM Compliance | Equipment Availability | Decision Basis |
|-------|------|---------------|--------------|----------------------|----------------|
| 1 | Reactive | Less than 50% | Less than 60% | Less than 80% | Run to failure |
| 2 | Developing | 50-65% | 60-75% | 80-85% | Calendar-based PM |
| 3 | Competent | 65-80% | 75-85% | 85-90% | Condition-based on critical assets |
| 4 | Optimizing | 80-90% | 85-92% | 90-95% | Risk-based, data-driven decisions |
| 5 | World-Class | Greater than 90% | Greater than 92% | Greater than 95% | Predictive analytics, continuous improvement |

Full maintenance culture transformation from reactive to predictive typically requires 2.5 to 3 years when properly executed. Quick wins are achievable within six months on pilot assets. A pilot means selecting a small group of assets (e.g., three pumps at one station) to prove the approach before scaling to the entire utility.

### Documented Utility Transformation Results

| Utility | Starting State | Results | Timeframe |
|---------|---------------|---------|-----------|
| Metropolitan Sewer District of Greater Cincinnati | 46% proactive work, 55% emergency failure rate | Proactive work over 70%, emergency failures down 55%, reliability up 33.6%, $5 million annual savings combined with Greater Cincinnati Water Works | 2 years (2011-2013) |
| Columbus Department of Public Utilities, Ohio | Historically-based planning, delayed capital improvements until failure | $55 million in avoided capital and O&M costs from $7 million investment (8:1 ROI) | 2008 onward |
| Boston Water and Sewer Commission | Non-revenue water at 48% | Non-revenue water dropped to 15-18%, 34-35 pipe failures per year for system of 1,000+ miles | Multi-year, accelerated by EPA Consent Decree 2012 |
| Southern Water, United Kingdom | 70% reactive maintenance, 40% error margin in asset data | 100,000 assets surveyed, over 20,000 standardized operating procedures, 500+ staff trained, 84% workforce confidence score | Multi-year program documented 2024 |

Non-revenue water is water that is produced but never billed to customers, lost to leaks, theft, or metering inaccuracies. ROI (Return on Investment) compares the money saved against the money spent. An 8:1 ROI means $8 saved for every $1 invested.

## 4. Typical Data Collected by Water Utilities

### Current Practice vs. Potential State

| Data Category | What Most Utilities Collect | What Is Possible with Integration |
|---------------|---------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Asset registry | Spreadsheet or CMMS (Computerized Maintenance Management System), often incomplete or outdated | Geospatially referenced registry with condition scores, replacement cost, criticality ranking |
| Vibration | Route-based monthly or quarterly on critical pumps | Continuous wireless sensors on all rotating assets, trended automatically |
| Motor current | Panel ammeter readings during rounds | Continuous ESA (Electrical Signature Analysis) with automated fault detection and sideband analysis |
| Flow and pressure | SCADA historian at 15-minute to 1-hour resolution | 1-second or faster sampling for transient detection |
| Temperature | Handheld IR gun during operator rounds | Continuous RTD (Resistance Temperature Detector) or thermocouple on bearings, windings, seal housing |
| Oil analysis | Annual or biannual sampling | Quarterly sampling with automated particle count, ferrous density trending |
| Work orders | Paper or basic CMMS records, incomplete | Digital CMMS integrated with SCADA and condition monitoring, automated PM generation |
| Vibration analysis | Outsourced to contractor, quarterly reports | In-house centralized PdM team with real-time data access |

SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) is the system that monitors and controls pumps, valves, and other equipment in real time. ESA (Electrical Signature Analysis) analyzes the motor current waveform to detect electrical and mechanical faults. A CMMS is software used to schedule maintenance, track work orders, and manage spare parts.

## 5. Implementation Challenges Utilities Report

| Challenge | Description | Frequency Reported |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Organizational culture | Resistance to change from reactive firefighters, middle management bottleneck | Most commonly cited barrier |
| Workforce demographic gap | Median age 48, less than 10% under 24, 30-50% eligible to retire within 10 years | Consistently top 3 AWWA State of the Water Industry concerns |
| Incomplete asset data | No accurate inventory, unknown condition, 40% error margin in some utilities | Very common |
| Technology integration | SCADA, CMMS, condition monitoring, GIS not connected. Data in silos | Very common |
| Executive turnover | Loss of champion leader stalls program momentum | Reported in multiple case studies |
| Short-term budget cycles | Annual budget conflicts with 3-5 year transformation timeline | Universal |
| Staff training gap | No formal apprenticeship pipeline for maintenance technicians | WRF (Water Research Foundation) documented as industry-wide issue |
| Reacting to emergency | Firefighting rewarded. Proactive work not visible to management | Cultural norm in most utilities |

GIS (Geographic Information System) maps the physical location of pipes, valves, and other buried assets. A "data silo" means information is stored in one system (e.g., SCADA) that cannot communicate with another system (e.g., CMMS). This prevents cross-system analysis.

## 6. WRF Research Projects

WRF (Water Research Foundation) is a nonprofit organization that funds research for the water sector. Their projects produce tools, guides, and findings that utilities can apply directly.

| Project | Title | Key Output |
|---------|-------|------------|
| WRF 4313 | Performance Benchmarking for Effectively Managed Water Utilities | Excel-based benchmarking tool aligned with EUM Ten Attributes |
| WRF 4659 | Collaborative Utility Benchmarking in North America | Adapted Australia WSAA Asset Management Customer Value tool for North American use |
| WRF 4264 | Changing Organizational Culture to Promote Sustainable Water Operations | Guidebook for water utility sustainability champions. $575,833 investment (2013) |
| WRF 4206 | Water Sector Workforce Sustainability Initiative | Recruitment, selection, technical training, knowledge retention guidance |
| WRF 4975 | Real-Time Monitoring and Control | Executive summary available at waterrf.org |
| WRF 4422 | Decision Support for Asset Management | Executive summary available at waterrf.org |
| WRF 5239 | Optimizing Sensor Networks and Advanced Sensing for Water Systems | AI-enhanced predictive maintenance to minimize system downtime |
| WRF 5290 | Utility Case Studies on Digital Water | Call for utility case studies on asset management, predictive maintenance, capital planning |

A five-year, multi-million dollar WRF international research program on advanced condition assessment and failure prediction produced the finding that age is a poor predictor of actual condition of buried assets. This finding challenges age-based replacement strategies still common in the industry. In practice, a 50-year-old pipe in good soil may be in better condition than a 20-year-old pipe in aggressive soil.

## 7. AWWA Standards and Resources

AWWA (American Water Works Association) is the primary professional organization for water utilities in North America. They publish standards, manuals, and reports that define industry best practices.

| Resource | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| AWWA G400-24 | Utility Management System standard. Sets requirements for management consistency across the sector |
| AWWA M32 | Computer Modeling of Water Distribution Systems. Free excerpts available |
| Maintenance Management for Water Utilities (3rd Ed.) | Primary reference for maintenance program development, CMMS implementation, staffing |
| The Water Workforce | Strategies for recruiting and retaining high-performance employees |
| AWWA Utility Benchmarking Program (2025 Ed.) | Annual performance indicator results. Metrics include planned maintenance %, corrective maintenance hours per MG, breaks per 100 miles, training hours per employee |
| 2017 Leading Business Practices in Asset Management | Case study report from 13 utilities selected from 545 surveyed |
| Journal AWWA | Peer-reviewed articles on water sector technology, operations, management |
| Opflow | How-to articles and case studies for operators |
| AWWA State of the Water Industry Report (Annual) | Industry trends, concerns, benchmarking data |

A standard (like G400-24) sets minimum requirements that a utility should meet. A manual (like M32) provides guidance on how to implement a specific practice. Benchmarking means comparing your utility's performance metrics against those of peer utilities to identify improvement opportunities.

The 2017 case study report selected 13 leading practitioners from 545 surveyed utilities. Portland Water Bureau (2,330 miles of pipe, 41 pump stations, $8 billion replacement value) reported benefits including avoiding high-consequence failures, performing more maintenance on high-consequence assets and less on low-consequence ones, and selecting alternatives with lowest life-cycle cost.

| Quantity | Formula | Units |

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---------|-------------|
| Treating PdM implementation as a technology project rather than a culture change project | Technology installed, but no behavior change. CMMS becomes expensive filing cabinet. Returns never materialize |
| Implementing predictive maintenance on all assets simultaneously instead of starting with pilot on worst asset | Overwhelms staff, produces no visible wins, program loses credibility and stalls |
| Rewarding firefighters who respond to emergencies while expecting proactive culture to grow | Staff optimize behavior for what is rewarded. Firefighting continues. Proactive work declines |
| Deploying condition monitoring without defined P-F intervals and action thresholds | Data collected but no decision framework exists. Alarms ignored. No maintenance action triggered at Point P |
| Purchasing sensors and software before cleaning up asset registry and work order data | Garbage-in-garbage-out. Predictive models trained on incomplete or inaccurate data produce unreliable results |
| Underinvesting in middle management training assuming senior leadership support is sufficient | Employees receive change messages from direct supervisors. Unprepared middle managers become bottleneck |
| Assuming 1:1 dollar savings ratio on PdM investment | First year results are modest. ROI compounds over 3-5 years as baseline reliability improves |
| Implementing condition monitoring without integrating data into existing CMMS workflow | Technicians must access separate system to see alarms. PM optimization never occurs |
| Using age as primary criterion for pipe replacement decisions | WRF research shows age is a poor predictor of actual condition of buried assets. Leads to premature or delayed replacement |
